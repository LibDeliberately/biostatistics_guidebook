Example Calculation 1: SRS Without Replacement

When sampling from a population of a known, finite size, we can estimate information about that population by considering an outcome in our sample and applying a correction.

## Information "given" by an experiment ##
# Population size from which we're sampling
N1 <- 55234

# Sample we had access to
n1 <- 500

# Number within sample who had our trait of interest
flag <- 44

## Calculations to use a finite-population correction for an SRS ##
# Proportion of those with flag in sample
prop <- flag/n1

# Determining weight
w <- 1/prop

# Computing the sample variance estimate for our binary flag variable
sSquare1 <- (n1/(n1-1))*(prop*(1-prop))

# Computing the standard error with the finite population correction for sampling without replacement
SEwor <- sqrt((1-(n1/N1))*(sSquare1/n1))

# Constructing the 95% CI
lb1 <- prop - qnorm(.975)*SEwor
ub1 <- prop + qnorm(.975)*SEwor

# And outputting the confidence interval...
c(lb1,ub1)
[1] 0.06325646 0.11274354
# the standard error...
SEwor
[1] 0.01262449
# and the proportion of sample with the flag
prop
[1] 0.088

From this calculation we could conclude…

  • “In a simple random sample without replacement, the estimated proportion of _____ who ______ was 0.088 (8.8%) with a standard error of 0.01262 (95% CI: 0.06326, 0.1127)”

Example Calculation 2: SRS With Replacement

When a selected observation can be picked again following its sampling, we don’t need to apply the finite-population correction.

# Calculating a new standard error without the finite population correction
SEwr <- sqrt(sSquare1 / n1)

# Constructing our new 95% Confidence Interval
lb1 <- prop - qnorm(.975)*SEwr
ub1 <- prop + qnorm(.975)*SEwr

# Outputting our values
c(lb1,ub1)
[1] 0.06314369 0.11285631
SEwr
[1] 0.01268202

From this calculation we could conclude…

  • “In a simple random sample with replacement, the estimated proportion of _____ who ______ was 0.088 (8.8%) with a standard error of 0.01268 (95% CI: 0.06314, 0.1129)”
